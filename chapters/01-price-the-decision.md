# A Good Investment the Firm Cannot Yet Make

Two compressors need replacing. The controller can buy a compact unit or convert the entire cooling bay; the same room cannot take both. The larger project looks better on the value sheet and fails on the cash sheet. Find the point at which those statements stop contradicting each other.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to rank two competing projects by discounted value, test a separate funding constraint, and say which assumption would change the recommendation. All numbers below are invented.

## Start with the payment dates

A frozen-food distributor makes one decision about one cooling bay. Compare two three-year alternatives with doing neither. Each projected operating cash flow is incremental and after tax. It arrives at the end of the named year, not when an order is booked. The year-three sale receipt is additional, net of selling costs and any taxes from the sale. There is no working-capital change, no financing charge in the cash flows and no cash flow after year three. Both alternatives have the same risk for this exercise, so use a 10% annual rate. Ignore rounding until the end.

| Time | Cash-flow component | Compact unit ($) | Whole bay ($) |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| Today | Purchase and installation | −160,000 | −240,000 |
| End of year 1 | Operating cash flow | 65,000 | 100,000 |
| End of year 2 | Operating cash flow | 65,000 | 100,000 |
| End of year 3 | Operating cash flow | 65,000 | 100,000 |
| End of year 3 | Sale receipt | 10,000 | 30,000 |

The distributor has $300,000 of unrestricted cash today. Its board insists that at least $90,000 remain immediately after the purchase. No financing or reserve exception has been approved. In this case the specified restriction is only a day-zero screen; the table has no evidence about cash availability on intervening days.

**Attempt before reading on.** Use a calculator or paper. For each option, write the discounted value of the operating and sale cash flows, then subtract the day-zero payment. On a separate line, write the cash balance immediately after paying. Record both rankings: which has the higher NPV, and which, if either, can be bought today?

## Take the apparent contradiction apart

Discounting at 10% converts a cash receipt at the end of year 3 to a present value by dividing it by 1.10 cubed. That applies to the sale receipt as well as the operating cash in that year. The purchase already happens today, so its discount factor is 1.

| Option | NPV rounded to nearest dollar | Unrestricted cash just after purchase | Passes today's reserve? |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Compact unit | $9,159 | $140,000 | Yes |
| Whole bay | $31,225 | $60,000 | No |
| Neither | $0 | $300,000 | Yes |

The whole bay creates about $22,066 more present value than the compact unit under these assumptions. It also leaves the firm $30,000 below the required reserve. The first result concerns value. The second concerns feasibility. The future receipts explain why the whole bay has a higher NPV; they cannot supply cash at the purchase date.

Of the options now authorized in the problem, choose the compact unit. If obtaining additional cash or negotiating a different payment schedule is genuinely possible, reopen the larger proposal. First check timing and restrictions, then recompute the economics under the changed terms. A $30,000 loan could meet an arithmetic cash test if all proceeds were unrestricted and fees were zero; the case gives no price, borrowing capacity, repayment or covenant data to conclude it is a good deal. Nor does the simplified reserve screen stand in for a rolling liquidity forecast.

> Simulated response: Choose the whole bay. It leaves $60,000 after installation, but the first year's $100,000 fills the reserve and makes the project feasible.

Then compute the minimum unrestricted opening cash for the larger project: it must cover both the $240,000 payment today and the $90,000 cushion immediately afterward.
