# Forecast the Bank Balance, Not the Month

At the start of a month the distributor's treasurer is comfortable. At the end of the month she expects to have more cash than the company normally requires. Her operations manager wants to use that end-of-month number to authorize spending today. The treasurer asks a narrower question: what's the first date on which it might be impossible to make a scheduled payment and still keep enough cash on hand?

By the end of this chapter, explain why a positive balance at the reporting date is not a funding plan, reconcile the dated movements, and identify the first action that would close a shortfall.

## Name the forecast before opening it

Chapter 1's compact cooling unit leaves the company with $140,000 immediately after purchase. Now the treasury team applies a **separate** operating policy: retain $90,000 at the end of each of the next four weeks. The decision in this chapter is whether the firm needs a cash remedy in the first month, not whether the original purchase increased value. The weekly cash amounts below cover the firm, not just the project. They belong to the same fictional company, but cannot simply be added to Chapter 1's incremental annual cash-flow valuation.

There are at least three clocks here. The customer signs an order. The seller delivers and bills. The bank receives cleared cash. A dated liquidity forecast uses the relevant payment clock. For another purpose, the accounting recognition date can be important. Do not correct a cash schedule by making it match the income statement.

When a company publishes a cash flow statement, it also distinguishes operating, investing and financing flows. That statement is a useful historical reference, not a calendar of future payroll or customer receipts. Compare the [SEC's overview of financial statements](https://www.sec.gov/investor/pubs/begfinstmtguide.htm). A short-term forecast needs dated evidence such as the collection terms, the current bank balance, and the settlement dates for the largest disbursements.

## Take the forecast apart

**Attempt first.** Fill the balance column without using an AI tool. Mark the first failing date and the maximum shortfall below the $90,000 floor at a weekly measurement date. Now predict what happens to the week-four balance if $20,000 of the week-two collections arrive in week one instead, leaving all other cash flows unchanged. Put both forecasts in separate columns; do not overwrite the first one.

The four closing balances are 75, 103, 116 and 121. The opening balance plus aggregate collections of 139 minus aggregate payments of 158 gives 121, an independent total check. It is not the liquidity check. The week-one balance misses the floor by 15; looking only at week four hides the violation.

## Find a remedy, not a better-looking cell

Acceleration of $20,000 from week two to week one moves week one's ending cash to $95,000. Week two and the terminal balance are unchanged. The change is a reassignment in time; it is not a new sale. The forecast works only if the earlier receipt clears. A customer email saying “we will try” and a confirmed change in settlement date are not equivalent inputs. If the customer asks for a discount to accelerate, record the fee as an actual amount and date before re-evaluating the trigger.

A one-row cash forecast can also overstate precision. A day-by-day example within week one: payroll is due Tuesday and a receipt arrives Friday. Even if Friday clears the floor, payroll day may not. The weekly exercise tests only the weekly policy. If a daily dollar floor or payment deadline is binding, disaggregate the relevant week before taking an action.

## Cash tied up in operations

A different case window illustrates why a profitable operation can absorb cash while it grows. Operating working capital in this example is receivables plus inventory less trade payables. It rises from 92 (66 receivables + 70 inventory − 44 payables) to 107 (72 + 80 − 45). If there are no noncash remeasurements, unusual classification changes or other working-capital components in this simplified example, the increase of 15 absorbs cash relative to operating income on the specified basis. It does not tell you which weekday the cash moves. Neither working capital nor the current ratio gives you the dated payment schedule.

**Inspect the answer.** A simulated analyst writes, “Ending cash is 121. The company has 31 free to distribute this month.” Locate the unsupported leap. Even if week four exceeded its floor, the forecast tells you neither that interim restrictions were met nor what next month's liabilities are. A floor is a constraint, not a definition of distributable cash.

**Transfer.** Move only $14,000 of the second week's collections into the first. Is the floor met? What evidence would a treasurer request before relying on a $15,000 acceleration? Write a rule naming a date, a balance, the responsible person, and the alternative if the receipt does not clear.
