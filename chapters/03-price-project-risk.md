# The Rate Is a Claim About Risk

The plant team tells the finance committee its favored project loses if the risk assessment changes. That's useful to know. It does not justify choosing whichever rate makes the favored project win.

This chapter teaches you to separate projected dollar amounts from the return demanded for bearing risk. The calculation is a sensitivity, not a prescription for the right cost of capital.

## Find what the model is holding fixed

Chapter 1 priced both alternatives at 10%. The finance committee now believes the whole-bay project might be more exposed to downturns in the same markets that affect its investors. Leave the estimated whole-bay cash amounts untouched for the comparison. This deliberately isolates what rate changes do. A different issue, such as an equipment installation failure, might instead change the amounts or probabilities in the forecast. A risk should not be charged once in the cash amounts and again by an unsupported rate penalty.

Hold the compact unit at 10%. Use the whole bay's original $240,000 investment, $100,000 operating receipts at the ends of years one through three, and $30,000 sale receipt in year three. Estimate the whole bay's discount rate by asking what return an investor would require for a comparable claim, not by asking what rate the company happens to pay on a loan.

**Attempt first.** Put the options at 10%, only then revalue the whole bay at 14% and 16%. Identify the first scenario where its NPV falls below the compact unit's. Treat the cash reserve test separately. A change in the demand for risk bearing changes present value, not the number of dollars due now.

| Whole-bay discount rate | Whole-bay NPV (nearest dollar) | Compact unit NPV (nearest dollar) |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 10% | $31,225 | $9,159 |
| 14% | $12,412 | $9,159 |
| 16% | $3,809 | $9,159 |

If all earlier assumptions remain in force and both alternatives have become feasible, the relative rank reverses between the last two rows. The larger proposal's standalone NPV is still positive at 16%; that does not make it the best use of the same bay. If neither option were permitted by the funding constraint, neither ranking would settle the decision.

## Ask what earns the extra return

A coupon is a payment specified by a financing contract. A discount rate is an assumption about the return required to value a particular stream of cash. The latter should match what is being valued. Investors can ordinarily diversify some project-specific surprises, whereas a project's sensitivity to broad economic returns may persist in the context of a diversified portfolio. Market-based approaches try to distinguish those risks. See [William Sharpe's original capital-asset-pricing paper](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1964.tb02865.x) for one model of this distinction, not for a current rate for this company.

Comparables do not produce precision automatically. Ask which business activities actually match, whether financing differences distort observed equity risk, what currency and inflation convention the inputs use, and which period the data cover. The rate at which this company can finance part of the project is not evidence that the project itself is a low-risk claim. Nor is every high-variance project necessarily more exposed to broad market risk.

A defensible memo states what was changed, what was left unchanged, and why the evidence points one way rather than the other. “The larger project wins at any rate that has been defended” is much narrower than “the larger project wins.”

## Build a rate from its claims, not its decimal places

A finance team may start with a weighted average cost of capital. In a deliberately simplified case, assume a stable target mix of 65% equity and 35% debt, measured at market value; required returns of 13.5% for equity and 6.4% for debt; and a usable marginal tax shield of 20% on interest in this model. The weights describe an ongoing funding policy, not the cash needed to pay one bill. The result is:

`0.65 × 13.5% + 0.35 × 6.4% × (1 − 0.20) = 10.567%`

The equity contribution is 8.775 percentage points. The debt contribution is 1.792 percentage points. Leave the rate unrounded for calculation; round only when communicating a precision the inputs justify. If that interest shield cannot be used and the other inputs are held fixed solely for sensitivity, the blended rate is 11.015%, not 10.567%.

This rate, if supported, prices an unlevered operating cash-flow forecast for activities with comparable business risk under the stated financing policy. It is not a promised yield on a particular claim. Before using it, ask whether the project's risk matches that of the assets behind the equity return, whether its currency matches the inputs, and whether its project cash flows omit payments to capital providers. If the policy cannot be maintained or the tax shield follows a very different timetable, a single blended rate can hide what changed. Separate the estimated operating value and the financing effects when that gives a clearer model.

There is another mistake hidden in the mix: a sponsor may assume debt's six-point interest rate stays low as the firm substitutes debt for almost all of its equity. That is not an inference from the example. Leverage changes the risk of the equity and may change what lenders require as well. The exercise changes one input for sensitivity; it does not establish a funding strategy.

## Do not smuggle a second penalty inside the model

A simulated memo says: “Scale made the whole bay riskier, so I used 16%. To be safe, I also subtracted an arbitrary 20% haircut from every receipt.” It may be sensible to consider a lower demand scenario. It is not sensible to report both adjustments as an independently justified response to the same poorly specified concern. Write separate candidate analyses: a demand assumption changes forecast receipts; a market-risk argument changes how forecast cash is priced. If you use both, document why their mechanisms are distinct.

**Transfer.** At 14% the whole-bay option is ahead if both are feasible. Compute the present value of cutting the sale receipt by $5,000 while keeping the discount rate fixed. Is that enough to reverse the rank? What would you need to know about the sale forecast before treating the reduction rather than the original as the expected receipt?
