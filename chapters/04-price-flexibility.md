# Buy Information Before Buying Equipment

The controller can keep next year's cooling-bay slot open, but the vendor wants an $8,000 nonrefundable fee now. The fee buys neither a compressor nor revenue. It buys time to see whether a prospective distribution agreement is signed before equipment orders lock. That can be worth money if the company is actually free to walk away.

You will value what the information changes, then test the contract that is supposed to make the change possible. This is a separate illustrative decision, not an automatic extension of the Chapter 1 investment figures.

## Put the choices at the same date

At the end of year one the firm can pay for the expansion or decline. Exercise values in the table are **at year one**, after subtracting the price to build and incorporating subsequent projected operating cash, taxes and sale cash. We are not given the build price separately and must not subtract a second price. In this example assume the alternative with no extra information can also place the order at year one. Assume the two choices have the same investment terms, except for the fee and information, and remain feasible in both modeled states. Treat the probabilities as valuation weights for this simple example and use 5% to bring the year-one amounts to today. The discounting and weights are stipulated, not a general way to price uncertain options.

| State at year one | Weight | Value at year one if expanding ($000) | Value if saying no ($000) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Agreement signs | 55% | 38 | 0 |
| Agreement fails | 45% | −25 | 0 |

**Attempt first.** Without advance information, choose one action for both states. Compute its year-one expected value, then discount. With information, you get to choose an action after learning the state. Compute that second value, discount, and subtract the fee today. Mark the decision points in ink before looking at the arithmetic.

Without the signal, ordering in every state is worth 0.55 × 38 + 0.45 × (−25) = 9.65 at year one. It beats declining in both states. Today's value is 9.65 / 1.05, about 9.19. With the signal, order when the agreement signs and decline when it fails; the year-one value is 0.55 × 38 = 20.90. Today's value after the fee is 20.90 / 1.05 − 8 = about 11.90. In this comparison the fee buys about 2.71 of net value. These are thousands of dollars.

## Ask what you are being sold

The difference is not created by forecasting the favorable state more confidently. It comes from applying a different decision in the unfavorable state. If the reservation contract instead obligates the firm to build, then learning the state does not create the modeled flexibility. The information may still have operational uses, but our calculation does not price those benefits.

The most the firm would pay for the signal in this scenario is the difference before fees, about 10.71. If the actual fee arrives later or has partial refunds, use the specific cash dates. If vendors can exercise discretion, contract terms may change, or the signal is poor, the table is no longer the model. An analyst cannot treat a potential renegotiation as a contractual option.

**Inspect the answer.** A supplied response says that 20.90 / 1.05 = 19.90 is the value added by the option. It forgot both the alternative worth 9.19 and the fee of 8 today. Where in the scenario do the two decisions differ? Use those differences, not the total value of a strategy, to justify the reservation.

**Transfer.** Change only the fee to 12 today. Does buying the signal still beat ordering without it? Then describe one additional real-world test that could matter even at the original fee, such as a signal that becomes public only after the vendor's decision deadline.
