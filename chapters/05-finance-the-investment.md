# Finance an Investment Without Disguising the Cost

The board sees a financing proposal with a low advertised rate. The loan is modest, the expansion has positive NPV, and the initial cash test now passes. How does a small loan create a large payment problem three years later? To answer, take the funding proposal out of the project valuation and draw its own cash timeline.

This chapter asks you to distinguish asset cash flows, lender claims and residual cash. You will use a payment schedule to identify a funding problem that the initial advance hides.

## Separate the asset from the financing

Suppose the distributor has conditionally reopened the whole-bay proposal. This is a hypothetical negotiation, not authority for a purchase. The project still requires $240,000 immediately and, in the original scenario, produces $100,000 operating cash at each year-end plus $30,000 net sale cash in year three. The asset has exactly the same receipts before we decide who supplies the money.

One lender is willing to advance $50,000, immediately unrestricted. It charges 6% each year on the $50,000 still outstanding and requires all principal back at year three. Interest is paid once each year. There are no fees or tax effects in this example. This type of terminal principal payment is not an accounting adjustment. It is an actual claim on that year's available cash. For a broader description of contractual debt cash flows, see the [SEC's Investor.gov corporate bonds overview](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/bonds-or-fixed-income-products/corporate-bonds); the loan here is fictional, not a bond offer.

| Date | Asset cash flow ($000) | Financing cash flow to the firm ($000) | Residual of these modeled flows ($000) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Order date | −240 | +50 | −190 |
| End of year 1 | +100 | −3 | +97 |
| End of year 2 | +100 | −3 | +97 |
| End of year 3 | +130 | −53 | +77 |

**Attempt first.** Rebuild the last column without reading it as NPV. What is cash immediately after order if opening cash is $300,000? Now suppose only $42,000 of unrestricted cash is available immediately before the lender must be paid in year three. What is the shortfall attributable to that payment alone?

The opening test now passes: 300 + 50 − 240 = 110, which is 20 above a 90 floor. The later payment is 53, so 42 available is 11 too little even before considering other commitments or a continuing reserve requirement. If a reserve also applies after debt service, that is a different and more demanding test. Do not count a sale receipt before confirming when it arrives; the debt payment may be due first.

## Test the claim that borrowing creates value

The advance is real cash, but so are the three payments. Discount the contract's payments at 6%, the same yield used to construct them, and the present value of the lender's payments to and from the firm nets to zero. This is an identity of the specified contract and discount rate, not evidence that every real loan has no economic benefits or costs. A fee, concession, permitted tax benefit, restriction or future shortage can matter. To value it, specify the relevant assumptions and discount each kind of cash flow consistently.

Do not remove interest and principal from asset flows and then discount the residual as though nothing about risk or financing changed. That residual has a different claimant and risk. If a model uses an asset valuation first, follow it with a transparent financing analysis rather than silently changing what the asset NPV means.

As an alternative, a new investor proposes $60,000 for 200 new units against 1,000 now outstanding. A $5,000 issue cost leaves $55,000 usable cash. Assume one class and equal proportional voting and distribution rights for this illustration. The new holder has 200 / 1,200, or one-sixth of the resulting units; there is no fixed principal payment to that holder in this exercise. The cost is not “free cash”: existing holders share decision rights and future distributions. Evaluate the issue price and terms, not dilution in isolation.

**Inspect the answer.** A model says the loan returns more value than equity because the debt costs only 6% and equity “costs” 16.7%. The percentage labels sound comparable. One is an annual contractual interest rate on principal; the other is the fraction of units owned after issuance. Ask the analyst to name the numerator, denominator and time unit of each percentage before comparing anything.

**Transfer.** If the lender withholds a $2,000 fee from proceeds and all promised payments stay the same, what opening cash and present value does the contract produce at the same 6% discount rate? Which failure in the year-three scenario does the lower opening balance not solve?
