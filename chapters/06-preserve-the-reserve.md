# Return Money Without Returning the Safety Margin

A profitable firm can disappoint owners by holding excessive cash. It can also disappoint them by distributing money needed at the next payroll. The payout proposal sounds precise: release $70,000 from an unrestricted opening balance of $220,000. The quality of that recommendation depends on which week the proposer stopped looking.

You will compute a maximum payout under a stated downside case and distinguish it from a suggested payout. You will also separate a change in fractional ownership from evidence of wealth creation.

## Put the payout on the downside calendar

Use a new fictional decision date. Before any new distribution the firm has $220,000 available. Under the tested downside scenario its cumulative cash uses exceed cash receipts by as much as $75,000 before recovering. This is a peak cumulative shortfall from the starting date, not an expense to subtract each week. The modeled floor after a distribution is $90,000 at the bottom of the path. Ignore all other constraints solely for this example.

**Attempt first.** Suppose the payment is today and reduces every forecast balance by the same amount. Work backward from the lowest balance. What payout leaves the floor just met? How much would the proposed payment miss by? Is a payout exactly on the floor automatically advisable?

The proposal can be written as a condition:

`220,000 − payout − 75,000 ≥ 90,000`

That caps payout at $55,000 in this simplified scenario. A $70,000 distribution leaves only $75,000 at the trough, $15,000 below the test. A $55,000 distribution leaves no room for a cash forecast miss. If the receipts used in the scenario could be delayed further, or the company has a more restrictive payment commitment, the proposed payout may need to be smaller than the computed ceiling.

The amount authorized is not the amount permanently safe. Make the condition repeatable by naming the person who refreshes the forecast, identifying the cash horizon, and stating what happens when a larger investment is approved or receipts fall behind. A distribution timing choice also matters. The result above assumes today, not at the end of the horizon after uncertainty clears.

## Compare two uses of the same amount

Consider an approved $30,000 cash outflow in a separate comparison. With 18,000 shares all receiving equal distributions, a cash dividend would be $30,000 / 18,000 per share, about $1.67. We ignore taxes, transaction costs and market response. In an alternative, assume the firm pays $30 each for 1,000 shares and removes them from the outstanding count. A continuing holder of 180 shares owns 1.00% before the repurchase and about 1.06% afterward.

The continuing holder's share of the firm is larger. The firm also spent $30,000. A larger fraction is not enough information to say the holder is richer. The repurchase price relative to underlying value, who was able to sell, expected returns on retained cash, and the total cash position all matter. If the cash is actually needed for a higher-value investment or to avoid a future financing cost, both payout forms may be poor choices. This teaching comparison makes no claim about a jurisdiction's tax or corporate law.

**Inspect the answer.** A supplied memo recommends a payout because “the balance exceeds the threshold today,” then recommends a repurchase because “the remaining ownership percentage increases.” Find the missing forecast in the first argument and the missing value comparison in the second. Preserve the true observations rather than rejecting everything in the memo.

**Transfer.** Suppose the same downside cash uses instead reach $90,000 and the board separately protects $15,000 for a scheduled cash expense already confirmed to fall before the forecast trough. Compute the ceiling assuming the expense is not yet in the forecast. How does the answer change if it was already included?
