# Buy a Business Without Paying for Your Own Work

The acquisition target has a distribution route the buyer wants. The broker says the buyer should pay a premium because the target will be worth more in the buyer's hands. That tells you a benefit might exist. It does not tell you how much of that benefit you should pay to the seller.

You will arrive at a price limit from dated present values, then identify which assumptions could invalidate it. The numbers are synthetic and expressed on one valuation date.

## Build the bridge before discussing a premium

Assume all amounts refer to comparable claims on an agreed transaction basis. No extra debt, cash or financing adjustment is needed in this example. If a real analysis starts with operating enterprise value and the contract states a price for equity, bridge the claims and any debt-like obligations before comparing numbers.

A standalone value of $390,000 estimates what the target's cash flows are worth absent the buyer's incremental changes. The buyer estimates an additional $95,000 of present value from routes that can overlap under common scheduling; that estimate does not include integration costs. Those costs have a separate $44,000 present value. The proposal is to pay $420,000 immediately. All pieces are after tax where applicable and priced on a consistent illustrative basis.

| Component | Value on the chosen date ($000) | Why it is included |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Standalone business | +390 | Cash flows the buyer would acquire without the improvements |
| Additional value from the combination | +95 | Incremental cash from changes not available in the standalone baseline |
| Integration costs | −44 | Required spending not in the two value lines above |
| Payment to sellers | −420 | Cash the buyer must give up today |

**Attempt first.** Calculate the buyer's NPV. Then put a different unknown purchase price into the final row and solve for the price at which the result is zero. Identify what part of the total would be counted twice if a standalone appraiser had already assumed route consolidation.

The resulting NPV is $21,000. A zero-NPV setting of the price is $441,000. It is a ceiling under the model, not a recommended offer. If a feasible alternative opportunity already produces $25,000 of NPV and uses the same scarce team time, the transaction in the table does not beat that alternative at $420,000. The opportunity cost matters even where available cash would cover the price.

## Turn the diligence question into a decision rule

Someone on the operations team can tell you whether routes overlap. Someone on the customer team can tell you which customers are allowed to leave. The model owner needs both. If the revised synergy value is 49 rather than 95, holding other figures unchanged, the proposal falls to −25. A price ceiling is meaningful when the deciding party knows the facts that update it.

The best counterargument is not “every acquisition fails.” Ask whether the available schedule is big enough to support the synergy estimate after allowing for volume losses, cost commitments and implementation delays. Test whether the cash assumptions survive independently rather than giving different names to the same optimism.

**Inspect the answer.** A prepared memo adds the full $95,000 to a target valuation which already includes $25,000 of the same overlapping routes. Keeping everything else constant, how much fell into two lines? What is the corrected buyer NPV if the standalone baseline already contains that $25,000 and the extra estimate is otherwise accurate?

**Transfer.** A candidate integration plan costs another $12,000 today but protects $20,000 of expected present-value synergies that would otherwise be lost. Compare those two marginal contributions. Explain why a positive incremental result on integration does not, by itself, settle whether the acquisition should go forward.
