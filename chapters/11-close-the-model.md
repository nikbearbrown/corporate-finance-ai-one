# Close the Model Before Forecasting It

The treasurer sees the company earn $50,000 and the bank balance fall by $6,000. The controller says there is no discrepancy. There might be one, but the two figures alone cannot reveal it. Reconstruct the records that explain both outcomes, then find a transaction whose effects cannot be hidden by a balancing plug.

This case teaches you to link an income statement, a cash-flow schedule and a balance sheet. The distributor here uses a fresh fictional dataset, not a continuation of the exact balances in the earlier chapter. All amounts are thousands of dollars. Classification assumptions are supplied for this teaching model. They are not a claim about the presentation every reporting framework requires.

## Get the opening ledger right

| Opening balance | Amount | Opening claim | Amount |
| --- | ---: | --- | ---: |
| Unrestricted cash | 170 | Payables to suppliers | 60 |
| Receivables | 80 | Debt | 120 |
| Inventory | 100 | Equity | 470 |
| Net equipment | 300 | | |
| **Total** | **650** | **Total** | **650** |

During the period, the firm sells 360 on credit. Cost of goods sold is 204, salaries are 60, depreciation is 22, interest is 8 and taxes are 16. This model assumes salaries, interest and taxes are paid as recorded, and interest and taxes count in the teaching schedule's operating cash. There are no other revenues, expenses, write-offs or accounting changes.

Ending receivables are 112. Ending inventory is 121. Ending trade payables are 65. All inventory purchases move through those payables. Capital spending of 35 is paid in cash with no asset disposals. New borrowing is 20 with no principal repayments, and a cash distribution of 15 goes to owners. No equity issuance or other equity movements occur.

**Before looking below**, sketch four roll-forwards: receivables, inventory, payables and equipment. For each, identify the item you cannot directly see in the income statement. Then build both income and operating cash. Use the known closing noncash balances to verify the result rather than inputting a calculated cash balance as an unexplained plug.

## Follow the missing cash from account to account

Operating income is 360 − 204 − 60 − 22 = 74. Interest takes that to 66 before tax, and net income is 50. The income statement has done its job. It has not explained collections.

Receivables increase from 80 to 112. Start at 80, add credit sales of 360, and remove collections. Therefore collections are 328. Inventory increases from 100 to 121 even after shipping goods with carrying cost 204. The company must have purchased 225. That was not necessarily the cash it paid. Opening trade payables plus purchases, less payments, equal closing trade payables. Payments are 60 + 225 − 65 = 220.

Direct operating cash is 328 − 220 − 60 − 8 − 16 = 24. A different route reaches the same result: start with 50 of net income, add back 22 of depreciation, deduct 32 more in receivables and 21 more in inventory, then add the 5 increase in trade payables. This bridge is valid given the supplied account movements. A change in a balance-sheet line could have other causes in a real model, such as an acquisition or currency translation. Do not attach a cash interpretation until you know why the balance moved. See the [SEC's overview of the statements](https://www.sec.gov/investor/pubs/begfinstmtguide.htm) for the broad distinction. The balances and reporting choices here are synthetic.

## Verify from a different direction

Investing cash is −35. Financing cash is +20 − 15 = +5. The change in cash is 24 − 35 + 5 = −6, so cash closes at 164. Equipment closes at 300 + 35 − 22 = 313. Debt closes at 140 and equity at 470 + 50 − 15 = 505.

| Closing balance | Amount | Closing claim | Amount |
| --- | ---: | --- | ---: |
| Cash | 164 | Trade payables | 65 |
| Receivables | 112 | Debt | 140 |
| Inventory | 121 | Equity | 505 |
| Net equipment | 313 | | |
| **Total** | **710** | **Total** | **710** |

A balanced balance sheet is a useful check, not certification that all assumptions are right. A mistaken transfer between two perfectly measured accounts can preserve equality. The most informative cross-check of collections here is that you can get the same 24 from dated inflows and outflows or from a reconciled bridge starting at net income. Check actual supplier invoices and bank dates in real work, not the identity alone.

A supplied memo says: “Adding depreciation raised cash by 22.” The depreciation adjustment reverses a noncash expense when reconciling profit to cash. It is not a cash receipt. There may be a tax consequence in another analysis, but taxes in this case are already supplied and paid at 16. Creating a new cash flow would double count.

## Change one transaction, change only what it touches

A customer reschedules 12 of collections into the next period without a pricing change, evidence of loss or other side effect. Sales and profit stay as modeled. Receivables rise to 124, operating cash falls to 12 and closing cash becomes 152. Assets still total 710 because one asset was substituted for another. A lender who can see only leverage at period-end might miss the short-term consequence. A treasury policy triggered by the bank balance would not.

**Transfer A.** Instead, management delays payment of 9 to suppliers and all other assumptions hold. Recalculate operating cash, closing cash and payables. Name the next-period reversal the forecast must show.

**Transfer B.** The original accounts team accidentally categorizes the new borrowing as customer receipts. Without changing total cash, show how operating and financing cash are misstated. Which account reconciliation would reveal the mistake even if cash and the balance sheet totals still agree?

**Decision record.** Write the opening balance, two independent check routes, the one item you would reconcile to source records, and the consequence of a delayed receipt. A model critique may flag an inconsistency after your attempt; its recommendation is not independent evidence.
