# Value the Claim You Can Buy

The valuation team has one number for the operating company. The board has another for the price of its shares. The two figures look incompatible until you ask which claims each number includes. A manager who pays operating value for the equity may agree to service the acquired debt too. Work through the bridge before commenting on the price.

This lab introduces an enterprise-versus-equity valuation with a finite explicit forecast and a continuing-value estimate. You will calculate the cash-flow value, bridge it to a price per share in a stylized case, and test what assumption the estimate relies on most. All inputs are fictional; this is not a valuation of a security.

## Price the operating activities on one basis

Assume a continuing distribution business with the following free cash flow to the firm: 40, 47, 55 and 62 thousand dollars at the ends of years one through four. Those amounts reflect operating taxes, operating reinvestment and changes in working capital; they are before any distributions to debt or equity. Those same cash-flow definitions apply beyond the forecast. The case's required return for this stream is 10%. Treat all amounts and rates as nominal dollars.

At the end of year four, the business is still operating. The case assumes future cash grows at 2.5% from the year-four level, after allowing for the reinvestment that the particular growth forecast would require. This implies year-five cash of 62 × 1.025 = 63.55. For this particular perpetuity, the price of that stream at the end of year four is 63.55 / (0.10 − 0.025), about 847.33. That quantity does not belong at time zero. It must be discounted four periods.

**Attempt first.** Calculate two time-zero values, one for the four forecast years and one for continuing cash flows. Now add them together to get operating value. Mark where you used year-four cash, and where you used the year-five cash. Using 62 in both places is not the stipulated forecast.

| At time zero | Value ($000) |
| --- | ---: |
| Explicit four-year cash flows | 158.88 |
| Continuing cash flows | 578.74 |
| **Operating enterprise value** | **737.62** |

Rounded numbers can differ from a sum of individually rounded figures. Here the year-five starting flow belongs only in the continuing stream, and year-four flow belongs only in the explicit forecast. Do not add the first continuing-year payment again after computing the perpetuity.

## Decide what the transaction actually conveys

Suppose lenders have a claim worth 160 on the valuation date. In this exercise the business also holds 28 of excess cash that is not needed for the operating forecast, and is unrestricted, available to equity, and absent from operating value. There are no other debt-like obligations, nonoperating assets or priority classes. Equity value is 737.62 − 160 + 28, or 605.62 thousand dollars. With 40,000 equivalent shares, this is about $15.14 per share. Do not divide thousands of dollars by thousands of shares twice.

If the deal instead leaves some liability with the seller, or if cash is truly needed for operations, the bridge changes. An expected payment to settle old obligations is not removed simply because it is inconvenient; classify the rights transferred and consequences in the agreement. A complete real bridge can require far more than a line called “net debt.”

## Put the largest assumption where reviewers can see it

Approximately 78% of the modeled operating value comes from cash beyond year four. That is not inherently a reason to reject the estimate, but it tells you what sort of evidence would change it. At an 11% required operating return, with growth and explicit cash flows unchanged, operating value falls to about 647.74 and the per-share equity estimate to $12.89 given the same bridge. The model is sensitive to assumptions that are not facts.

Suppose a colleague offers another estimate from 8.5 × 90 of normalized EBITDA, yielding 765 of operating value. The metric, transaction basis, growth, margins, capital requirements, reference date and comparability of the chosen multiple still have to be checked. It does not become an independent validation simply because it uses multiplication instead of discounting. It may be calibrated from the same views of risk and future margins.

**Inspect the answer.** A draft multiplies the DCF operating value by the fraction of shares being bought and says debt need not be mentioned. Under the given all-equity-class assumptions, rebuilding from equity value is the required comparison. State what reliable debt and excess cash evidence would let you make the correction, and describe when proportional allocation to a minority position would itself need review.

**Transfer.** Repeat the case at 12%. Compare the reduction in explicit discounted flows with the reduction in continuing value. Write a board note that identifies the evidence that would improve the assumption driving the larger change. Do not report a single expected market price from this scenario analysis.
