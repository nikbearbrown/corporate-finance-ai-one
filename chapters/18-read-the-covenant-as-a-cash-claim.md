# Read the Covenant as a Cash Claim

The company owns a large restricted deposit. Its finance team shows plenty of balance-sheet cash and expects to pass a leverage covenant. The lender's definition does not count that deposit. A model that uses the correct cash total can still give the wrong contractual answer.

This fictional lab tests a leverage ratio under a precise, invented contract. It is not a general description of lender rights, remedies or the terms of any actual borrowing.

## Copy the specimen definition before doing finance

At the test date gross qualifying debt is 260 thousand dollars. There are 30 of eligible unrestricted cash and a further 55 in a restricted account that the assumed contract excludes. The numerator is qualifying gross debt minus eligible cash. The denominator is the contract-defined earnings measure; do not substitute operating cash or a standardized headline EBITDA. Assume the selected test passes if the multiple is **no greater than** 3.5.

At first, contractual earnings for the test period are 78. In the downside case they fall to 60 while all debt and cash amounts stay fixed. Ignore other covenants, taxes and restrictions except where explicitly added below. For both cases the same test date and measurement basis apply. Availability of debt cash at other dates remains a separate question.

**Attempt first.** Compute the numerator, then the ratio in both scenarios. Express “headroom” in dollars of additional allowable qualifying net debt at the baseline earnings. Do not subtract one ratio from another and report it as cash. Next calculate how much the qualifying net debt would have to fall to meet the downside test.

The measured net debt is 230. At 78 of contractual earnings the observed multiple is about 2.95, and permitted net debt is 3.5 × 78 = 273. Headroom is 43 under the specified definition. In the downside earnings scenario the multiple rises to about 3.83. Permitted net debt falls to 210, requiring a 20 reduction in the numerator, a recovery in the contractual earnings number, or a valid contract change.

## Spot the action that changes a gross line but not the ratio

A manager proposes spending 20 of eligible cash to repay 20 of qualifying debt. In the simplified covenant, qualifying debt becomes 240 but eligible cash falls to 10. Measured net debt remains 230. The action may affect interest or future refinancing, but it does not fix the ratio at this test date. It also leaves less eligible liquidity.

Another case proposal is to raise 20 of new unrestricted equity cash and use it immediately to repay qualifying debt, with no fees and with the original 30 eligible cash remaining. Gross debt becomes 240, measured net debt becomes 210 and the leverage test is met at equality. That is an arithmetic scenario, not evidence investors will commit the money or that an actual agreement permits the action, classifies the funds that way, or accepts this timing. Financing execution has to be verified. Recalculate if fees reduce the repayment or the funds arrive after the measurement date.

A lender could require more than this one condition. Even where net debt qualifies, scheduled payments may be impossible if cash cannot be released at a maturity date. Conversely a cash forecast that shows funds for a loan payment does not prove the covenant uses the same cash definition. Put the dated payment schedule and the agreement's measurement schedule side by side rather than forcing one to stand for both.

**Inspect the answer.** A simulated memo counts all 85 of cash and reports 175 / 60 ≈ 2.92. The arithmetic is internally consistent but the numerator uses the wrong claim on cash. Point to the clause in the case that changes the treatment, and describe which bank or ledger evidence would establish eligibility in a real evaluation.

**Transfer.** An unrelated entity offers to buy an idle asset for 23, proceeds net of fees and taxes. The added money will be unrestricted and eligible by the test date. Assume this does not change earnings for the specified test. Calculate measured net debt and the downside multiple before spending any proceeds. Then explain why a fair price, closing certainty and a separate valuation of lost future use would still matter.
