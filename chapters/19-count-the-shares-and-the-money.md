# Count the Shares and the Money

A planned offering is described as raising $630,000. Treasury wants to budget the full proceeds. One shareholder reads the same headline as confirmation that none of the new shares will transfer value. Both conclusions skip the money trail. Some shares are new, some were already outstanding, and not every dollar goes to the company.

This case teaches you to separate primary issuance, secondary sales, fees and share ownership. It is a mechanical counterfactual, not a prediction of market reactions or financial advice about any real offering.

## Follow the cash before updating the share count

A fictional company starts with 400,000 shares of one class. Its pre-offering equity value is assumed to be $2 million, or $5.00 per share. Assume that unchanged value for the preexisting equity claims before adding any incoming cash, ignoring taxes, signaling, changed operating value, order-book effects or demand creation. The offer price is $4.50.

The company issues 100,000 new shares and incurs $30,000 of its own issue costs. Some existing holders also sell 40,000 old shares at $4.50 in the same transaction. No sale proceeds from those old shares reach the company; ignore any separate seller charges for the example.

**Attempt first.** Draw three accounts for dollars: buyer, company and existing selling holder. Write down each flow. Then separately reconcile shares: opening shares, newly issued shares and resulting shares outstanding. Don't add a change in the identity of the holder to the total count.

| Quantity | Calculation | Result |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Gross primary proceeds | 100,000 × $4.50 | $450,000 |
| Fees paid by the company | Given | $30,000 |
| Net cash to the company | $450,000 − $30,000 | $420,000 |
| Proceeds paid to selling holders | 40,000 × $4.50 | $180,000 |
| Outstanding shares after issuance | 400,000 + 100,000 | 500,000 |

A new purchase of old shares relocates existing ownership. It does not create more units or fund a company expense. Treasury can plan on the mechanical $420,000 only under the stated sale and fee assumptions and after checking when the proceeds become available.

## Separate proceeds from per-share value

Holding the original business and other equity-value inputs fixed, addition of $420,000 in cash raises the assumed aggregate equity value to $2.42 million. Spread across 500,000 shares, the mechanical value is $4.84 per share. Before any sale of old shares, the incumbent block of 400,000 shares would then be worth $1.936 million rather than $2 million, a decrease of $64,000 in this counterfactual. The new block is modeled at $484,000 against a subscription price of $450,000, a $34,000 difference. The remaining $30,000 effect is fees; together they reconcile the $64,000 decrease.

This calculation deliberately isolates the primary issue. For a preexisting holder who also sells older shares, add their actual sale cash and make the before/after unit holdings explicit. Do not treat the selling holder's proceeds as money available to the firm or silently value those units at $4.84 in place of the actual $4.50 sale.

Two warnings belong on the valuation sheet. First, selling below the assumed underlying value can transfer value even as the firm receives new cash. Second, an analyst cannot simply declare the underlying value when it matters; changed operating prospects or the use of proceeds may change it. A change in the share-count denominator, a percentage share of the enlarged firm, and a change in marked wealth are not interchangeable quantities. Here the new block is 20% of the enlarged total, while shares outstanding increase by 25% relative to the old count.

**Inspect the answer.** A simulation says to add all shares sold to the denominator and total gross proceeds to equity value. Reconcile both errors by reconstructing closing company cash and demonstrating that the identity of secondary holders does not change aggregate shares.

**Transfer.** Keep the company issuing 100,000 new shares, the $30,000 company cost and the underlying $2 million pre-offering value. Solve for the primary issue price at which the mechanical post-offering per-share value still equals $5.00. State the comparability assumptions that prevent this from being an actual minimum issue price recommendation.
